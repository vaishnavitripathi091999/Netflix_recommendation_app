How the numbers were producedThe five Excel steps, reproduced. Every figure recomputes from the raw extract, so this page and the workbook agree.

Cleaning the raw extract

The raw sheet holds one row per subscriber-show pair: Subscriber ID, Show Name, Episodes Watched and Episode Length (m). Rows missing any of the four are dropped — a blank breaks both the product and the pivot. A fifth calculated column is then added.

Total Watch Time = [Episodes Watched] * [Episode Length (m)]

First rows of the cleaned sheet.

Pivoting into a subscriber × show matrix

Correlation needs two equal-length lists of numbers, so the long table is reshaped: Subscriber ID to Rows, Show Name to Columns, Sum of Total Watch Time to Values. Each column becomes a variable the correlation can consume — that pivot is the Subscriber records table.

First 10 of 2,000 subscriber rows. Values are minutes.

Correlating every pair with CORREL

Each pair of columns is fed to Excel's CORREL, which returns the Pearson coefficient. Copying the formula across a square grid of show names produces the correlation table.

=CORREL('Recommendation data'!C$2:C$2001, 'Recommendation data'!G$2:G$2001)

Reading it: above about 0.8 are the strong pairs worth recommending on; anything between roughly −0.05 and +0.05 is noise and means the two titles have no relationship.

Mapping the scatter plots

A coefficient alone can mislead, so each headline pair is plotted as an XY scatter — one dot per subscriber. The workbook holds two such charts; both are reproduced on the dashboard's Pair scatter card, where any pair can be selected.

Summarising with a descriptive table

Finally the pivot is re-cut as an average per title, sorted descending. This separates correlation signal from sheer popularity — a title can be heavily watched and still correlate with nothing.

What the analysis concluded

Source: Netflix Exercise - Raw Data.xlsx — 20,000 raw rows, 18,000 complete after cleaning, 2,000 subscribers × 9 titles, embedded in this page. No external requests. Method: total watch time = episodes watched × episode length; correlations are Pearson's r over the subscriber × title pivot, recomputed whenever the audience segment changes. Assumptions: a pair is called strong at r > 0.80; clusters join strong pairs that share a title; rows missing any source field are excluded rather than imputed. Note: independent analysis of a sample dataset, not affiliated with or endorsed by Netflix, Inc., and containing no real subscriber data.
