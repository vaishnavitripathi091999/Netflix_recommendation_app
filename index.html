Subscriber watch-time correlation study

Method in plain terms: every subscriber's total watch time is totalled per show (episodes watched × episode length), giving one row per subscriber and one column per show. For each pair of shows we measure how closely those two columns move together across all subscribers — Excel's CORREL, the Pearson correlation. A value near +1 means people who binge one also binge the other, so it is a good recommendation. Near 0 means watching one tells us nothing about the other.

The Excel workflow, reproduced. This page documents how the numbers on the recommender page were produced — the same five steps carried out in the workbook: clean the raw extract, pivot it into a subscriber × show matrix, run CORREL across every pair, plot the pairs that matter, and summarise. Every figure shown here is recomputed live from the raw file, so the page and the workbook agree.

Cleaning the raw extract

The raw sheet arrives as one row per subscriber-show pair with four columns: Subscriber ID, Show Name, Episodes Watched and Episode Length (m). Rows missing any of the four are dropped, because a blank breaks both the product and the pivot. A fifth calculated column is then added.

Total Watch Time = [Episodes Watched] * [Episode Length (m)]

Pivoting into a subscriber × show matrix

Correlation needs two equal-length lists of numbers, so the long table is reshaped: Subscriber ID to Rows, Show Name to Columns, Sum of Total Watch Time to Values. The result is one row per subscriber and one column per show — every column is now a variable the correlation can consume.

Pivot table — Sum of Total Watch Time

First 12 of subscriber rows. Values are minutes.

Correlating every pair with CORREL

With the pivot laid out as a grid, each pair of columns is fed to Excel's CORREL, which returns the Pearson coefficient. Copying the formula across a square grid of show names produces the triangular correlation table in the workbook.

=CORREL('Recommendation data'!C$2:C$2001, 'Recommendation data'!G$2:G$2001)

Reading the output: values above about 0.8 are the strong pairs worth recommending on; everything between roughly −0.05 and +0.05 is statistical noise and means the two shows have no relationship at all.

Mapping the scatter plots

A coefficient on its own can mislead, so each headline pair is plotted as an XY scatter — one dot per subscriber, their watch time for show A on the x-axis and show B on the y-axis. These are the two charts held in the completed workbook, redrawn here from the same columns.

Chart 1 — Dark and Stranger Things

A clear upward band: the pair moves together, so the recommendation holds.

Chart 2 — You and Big Bang Theory

A shapeless cloud: no relationship, so neither title predicts the other.

Summarising with a descriptive table

Finally the pivot is re-cut as an average per show, sorted descending. This separates the correlation signal from sheer popularity — a show can be heavily watched and still correlate with nothing.
